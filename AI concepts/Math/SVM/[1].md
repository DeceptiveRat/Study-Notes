Of course! That's a great question. The formula for the distance comes from a standard geometric principle for finding the shortest distance from a point (in this case, the origin) to a plane or a hyperplane.

Here's a step-by-step breakdown of the derivation.

---
## The Core Idea: Projection

The shortest distance from a point to a plane is always along a line that is perpendicular to the plane. We know from the previous discussion that the vector **w** is the **normal vector**, meaning it's perpendicular to the hyperplane.

Therefore, the distance from the origin to the hyperplane is the length of the **projection** of any vector from the origin to the plane onto this normal vector **w**.


Let's find this distance for a generic hyperplane first, and then apply it to H1 and H2.

---
## The Derivation

Let's find the distance to a generic hyperplane defined by the equation:
$$w^T x + b = c$$

1.  **Find a Point on the Hyperplane:** Let $x_p$ be any point that lies on this hyperplane. The vector from the origin to this point is simply $x_p$.

2.  **Define the Unit Normal Vector:** To project onto the direction of **w**, it's easiest to use its unit vector, which has a length of 1. The unit vector is:
    $$\hat{w} = \frac{w}{\|w\|}$$
    *(Note: $\|w\|$ is the magnitude or length of w, sometimes written as $|w|$)*

3.  **Calculate the Projection:** The distance is the dot product of the vector $x_p$ with the unit normal vector $\hat{w}$.
    $$\text{Distance} = |x_p \cdot \hat{w}| = \left| x_p^T \frac{w}{\|w\|} \right| = \frac{|x_p^T w|}{\|w\|}$$

4.  **Solve for $x_p^T w$**: Since we know $x_p$ is on the hyperplane, it must satisfy the hyperplane's equation:
    $$w^T x_p + b = c$$
    Rearranging this gives us:
    $$w^T x_p = c - b$$
    *(Note: Since the dot product is commutative, $w^T x_p = x_p^T w$)*

5.  **Substitute and Finalize:** Now we can substitute $(c-b)$ back into our distance formula:
    $$\text{Distance} = \frac{|c - b|}{\|w\|}$$

---
## Applying This to Your Example

Now we can use this general formula for your specific hyperplanes, H1 and H2.

### For Hyperplane H1

* The equation is $w \cdot x + b = 1$.
* In our general formula, the constant $c = 1$.
* Plugging this in:
    $$\text{Distance from origin to H1} = \frac{|1 - b|}{\|w\|}$$

### For Hyperplane H2

* The equation is $w \cdot x + b = -1$.
* In our general formula, the constant $c = -1$.
* Plugging this in:
    $$\text{Distance from origin to H2} = \frac{|-1 - b|}{\|w\|}$$

The formulas in your image, `(1-b)/|w|` and `(-1-b)/|w|`, are the same, just without the absolute value bars. In the context of an SVM, these often represent a *signed distance*, indicating which side of the origin the plane is on, but for a true geometric distance, the absolute value is required.
